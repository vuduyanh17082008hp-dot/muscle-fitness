# Vietnamese Flavor Architecture

## Core goal
Keep a dish recognisably Vietnamese while adjusting it for the user's goal. Vietnamese food is built on balance and freshness, not on a generic "rice plus chicken" template.

## Flavor architecture
The core balance is salty-umami, sour, a little sweet, fresh, and aromatic. Fish sauce supplies depth, lime or vinegar supplies lift, garlic and shallot supply aroma, chilli supplies heat, and fresh herbs supply brightness. A dish usually feels incomplete if one of these is missing.

## Broth-based dishes
Pho (phở), bún bò Huế, hủ tiếu and mì Quảng are noodle dishes built around broth, and they get their identity from a long-simmered aromatic broth (spice, charred aromatics). The broth carries most of the flavour with comparatively little added fat. Adjust the noodle portion, add more protein or vegetables, and keep the broth character.

## Rice and noodles
Rice and rice-noodle bases (steamed rice, broken rice or cơm tấm, vermicelli or bún, rice noodles) are the usual energy source. Treat portion as the main lever. Do not remove the base entirely when the user wants the dish to still feel like itself.

## Protein choices
Lean pork, chicken, fish, shrimp, eggs, and tofu all fit naturally. Grilled, steamed, or simmered preparations are common and keep identity intact. Fattier meat and fried versions are also traditional; offer lighter options as a choice, not a correction.

## Grilled vs fried approaches
Lemongrass-marinated grilled meat (the pork chop on cơm tấm, bún thịt nướng) gives smoky, savoury character with less added oil than frying. Where a dish is traditionally fried, an oven or air-fryer approach can preserve some of the crispness (fried spring rolls or chả giò versus fresh rolls or gỏi cuốn is the common choice); say it will be different.

## Herbs, acidity and heat
Herb plates (mint, basil, coriander, perilla), lime, pickled vegetables, and chilli add flavour and volume cheaply. Lean on these before adding sugar-heavy or oil-heavy sauces.

## Dipping and dressing sauces
Nuoc cham and similar sauces are high in flavour per spoon. Use them in measured amounts, and note that they can be sweet; adjust sweetness and quantity rather than removing them.

## Coach decision rules
- Name the dish's flavour pillars and keep at least the fish sauce, acid, and herbs.
- Adjust the base portion and protein before changing seasoning.
- Ask which dish they mean if the variant (soup, dry noodle, rice plate) changes the answer.

## Do not claim
- Do not flatten the dish to generic rice and chicken.
- Do not state exact calories or macros for named dishes; use the tools.
